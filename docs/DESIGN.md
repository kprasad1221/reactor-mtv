# Design research: a music-video television station

## Historical frame

The station interface draws primarily from **1981–1986 MTV**. Its video-direction catalogue can include work from the whole 1980s, including David Fincher's 1989 videos. Station graphics and the visual language of an individual music video are separate systems; a channel can contain a different kind of film without changing the interface's historical frame.

Early MTV was not a single neon palette or a permanent degraded-tape filter. Its recognizability came from a stable mark and broadcast structure that could host changing illustrations, textures, materials and short animated identifiers. This is the useful design principle for an interactive station: the viewer recognizes where they are while each channel can have its own identity.

Fred Seibert's primary archive describes the 1981–1983 station IDs as short animated pieces related to radio jingles and album-cover art. His account of the mark also describes the move from planned three- or four-second identifiers to ten-second pieces because of tape machinery. These are production decisions, not a generic recipe for an “80s aesthetic.” The archived 1981–1982 logo board shows many treatments of the same underlying mark.

Sources and visual references:

- [Fred Seibert: the MTV network IDs](https://fredseibert.com/post/68726321/the-mtv-network-ids) — original creative-direction context and archival reel.
- [Fred Seibert: the MTV logo examined](https://fredseibert.com/post/650652416/ten-years-after-the-mtv-logo-examined) — flexible identity and production constraints.
- [People's Graphic Design Archive: MTV logo, 1981–82](https://peoplesgdarchive.org/item/7874/mtv-logo-1981-82) — work credited to Pat Gorman, Frank Olinsky and Patti Rogoff, with creative direction by Fred Seibert.

This research uses primary written accounts, credit records and archival imagery. The following interface rules are design interpretations, not claims that every historical broadcast followed them.

## Interface rules

| Element | Direction for this experiment | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Television frame | A consistent 4:3 picture, compact station bug and readable lower third | Put the music video at the center; make channel identity immediately legible |
| Station identity | A stable wordmark with cut-paper geometry and changing graphic treatments in future IDs | Borrow the principle of a flexible station identity without copying the MTV mark |
| Color | Warm paper/ink outside the picture; limited teal, yellow and pink for station graphics | Keep one visual system instead of stacking unrelated retro motifs |
| Typography | Bold compact display lettering for identity; straightforward sans/monospace for information | Separate expressive graphics from controls the viewer must read |
| Channel change | A brief 180 ms tuning texture, disabled for reduced-motion preference | A modern interaction cue; this timing is an application choice, not an archival reconstruction |
| Video treatment | Leave generated picture and sound intact | Permanent scanlines, chromatic aberration or tape damage can obscure whether generation worked |
| Controls | Channel/style, source image, free signal, explicit generation and stop | Each action has an observable purpose; changing a channel is not permission to spend credits |
| Language | Direct broadcast/performance language: channel, source, direction, take | Establish this experiment's own identity rather than inheriting unrelated project copy |

Avoid defaulting to purple synthwave horizons, arcade menus, chrome word art, 1990s grunge, Y2K graphics or modern streaming-service cards. Those may be valid references elsewhere; mixing them here weakens the chosen period. The current interface is a restrained implementation of this direction, not a historical facsimile or final art direction.

## Director study and channel translation

A director's name is a research reference, not a sufficient model instruction. The prompt must describe composition, lighting, material, camera behavior, performance, editing and the relationship to the source image. Each take gets one readable visual action rather than an entire music-video treatment compressed into six seconds.

| Channel | Reference and credit | Visual grammar to carry into a prompt | Short-take treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cinematic noir | David Fincher, Madonna's **Express Yourself** (1989) | Monumental production design, sculpted light, controlled camera movement, performance with deliberate framing | A low medium-wide view moves forward as the source subject makes one clear gesture |
| Drawn into the video | Steve Barron directed a-ha's **Take On Me** (1985); Michael Patterson and Candace Reckinger created its animation | Live action and drawn space coexist; pencil hatching, line movement and a readable boundary between worlds | The same gesture continues as a diagonal paper boundary reveals a graphite world |
| Pop stage spectacle | Mary Lambert, Madonna's **Material Girl** (1985) | Theatrical frontal staging, star performance, artificial studio glamour and coordinated movement | A recognizable source subject is staged in a proscenium composition; one accent motivates a closer view |
| Frame-by-frame fantasy | Stephen R. Johnson, Peter Gabriel's **Sledgehammer** (1986), with Aardman and Brothers Quay animation contributions | Tactile objects, pixilation/stop-motion, physical transformations and visual jokes | A nearby handmade object assembles, moves on a musical accent and resolves into a final pose |
| Adventure on location | Russell Mulcahy, Duran Duran's **Rio** and **Hungry Like the Wolf** (1982) | Location as spectacle, purposeful movement, bold color and cinematic pop adventure | Track with the source subject, then reveal the surrounding location while preserving screen direction |
| Surreal after-hours | Tim Pope, the Cure's **Close to Me** (1985) and **Lullaby** (1989) | Intimate theatrical spaces, odd scale, expressive close framing and practical absurdity | A slow camera creep reveals one unsettling but playful change in the source setting |

The reference works are historically distinct. Fincher's later-century precision is not the same visual language as Patterson/Reckinger's drawing, and Johnson's tactile animation is not interchangeable with a digital morph. The channel mapping preserves those differences. Music direction supports each treatment through an original genre/instrumentation/rhythm proposal; the BPM values are implementation choices, not sourced measurements of the reference tracks.

## Prompt contract

The user selects a key such as `drawn-world`. The application constructs the prompt internally:

```text
MUSIC DIRECTION
[Original genre, tempo, instrumentation, musical phrase and accents]

VIDEO DIRECTION
<Picture 1> is the source image.
[Subject preservation, composition, materials, lighting, camera,
 one action or transformation, editing and ending]
```

The implementation is in `dist/src/styles.js`; full prompts are public source code, not secrets. The product does not ask users to export or edit them. The source is a reference image, not necessarily a fixed first frame: this allows a style to reinterpret the scene while preserving recognizable identity. H3 Omni accepts reference imagery and generates synchronized audio from the direction prompt; see its [model API](https://www.reactor.inc/models/fast-h3-omni/api).

## Live evaluation before further styling

Use one source across two clearly different channels. First establish that the connection, upload, generation, audio, playback and closure work. Then judge the result against the channel's material, light, camera and action rules. A style label alone is not a successful interpretation.

Only after that evidence should the station acquire more elaborate IDs, channel-specific graphics or a broader director catalogue. The next artistic question is whether the generated takes are distinguishable and directed, not how many visual effects the interface can display.
